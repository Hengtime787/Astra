Why Music?

There are so many skills, hobbies, and general things to learn in life. So why did I choose to focus on music for 6 weeks?

Well, for one, music is a very universally applicable skill. Think of the last time that you went a single day without listening to music. Chances are, that's only happened once or twice, if not never. Music is such a big part of our everyday lives.

My Background

Most people my age have some sort of experience with making music. Currently, I play Tenor Saxophone for Washington's Jazz and Concert Band. I used to take Piano lessons, but stopped when I was seven or eight years old (thanks C*vid), and I felt like now would be the perfect time to pick it up.

How I actually learned

Over the four years away from the piano, I forgot how to read Bass Clef. If you don't know what a Clef is, it's the thing circled in red on the sheet music. This was probably the easiest, yet most frustrating part of the whole process. See, I'm used to Treble Clef, which you play on the Saxophone, and on the right hand of the piano, but for the left hand, Bass Clef is used, and it's just a few pitches below treble clef, so I kinda had to re-learn how to read sheet music, but with the added pain of my previous knowledge stabbing me in the back.

After doing a bunch of research and practise (yes, freedom means I'm allowed to use British English) with Bass clef, I was comfortable with sight reading (playing a piece for the first time with no previous exposure) bass clef. Next, I started on the song. But hold up, I had to pick a song first!

What Songs do I choose

Here's a quick question for you: How many songs do you think exist worldwide? Okay. Take your number and multiply it by 50. That's probably how many songs. That was a long winded way of saying there's an estimated 100-200 MILLION registered songs in the world. That number is sure to grow in the future. Anyways, I had a LOT of options for a song to play. I made a little list in my handy Passion Project packet (PPP for short), and used an extremely unbiased and logical method called "Which one sounds cool". Looking back, it probably should've occured to me that the ones that sound the best are generally the more complex and harder to play ones. So I took the next logical course of action: ChatGPT (that was a joke). After I realised that that strategy was maybe a little bit flawed, I just looked on the sheet music site Musescore (I actually really hate the company's terrible fake subscription model, but that's a little too politically heated for this), and I found a really well made transcription of The Hardest Part by Olivia Dean, which is one of my favourite songs (highly reccomend her music, scan that QR Code if you want to learn more), that was marked as a beginner piece, and looked doable. I analyzed what key it was in (I don't know what it's called but I know that you do the C major scale), and practised the scales for it.

The Saxophone Part 🎷

A little after week 2, I decided that I wanted to introduce some other element into the song, and since I play the Tenor Saxophone, it was perfect. I used Musescore (oh no) again to transpose (change the music from one intrument key into another) the music to Bb, which allowed me to play it on Tenor Sax. I had the music printed out, but I didn't really practise it, and the music video was my first time playing that on the sax.

The Takeaway

What did I learn?

This passion project had a much larger influence on my life than I would have ever expected. It didn't just teach me how absolutely garbage I am at playing scales or arpeggios, it also helped me optimise my workflow for different kinds of unconventional practices (In British English, C is the Noun, and S is the verb). Before this project, I had a good organised and productive system for doing work on the computer, like coding, homework, and games various important tasks, but for things that can be more 'messy', I didn't really have good sytems in place. I had to set up a habit tracker and a new consistant practise schedule, whereas with one off tasks like homework, I can just muster up the courage to face algebra (alone!) for 20 minutes. The key take away from this project, for both you, the reader, and me, is that consistant practise is much harder to acheive than big practise sessions (this applies to instruments, sports, coding, and anything that requires skill!), but it is so much more effective, and helps you hone your organisation skills for later in life, where you'll be facing higher stakes than public humiliation, or a garbage saxophone tone.
